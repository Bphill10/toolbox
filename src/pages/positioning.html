Where “5% above market, 15% fewer leavers” comes from

It is a planning figure, and it should be replaced with Mastronardi’s own number as soon as the data allows. Four ways to get there, from quickest to strongest.

1. Read it from today’s data weeks 4 to 8, once the market data is in

Give every employee a market ratio (their pay ÷ the market median for their job and country). Then, job by job, compare turnover in jobs paid above market with jobs paid below. The slope of turnover against the ratio is the answer: how many fewer leavers each extra 5% of pay buys.

On this demo data, across the 30 jobs with at least 10 people: 17% fewer leavers overall, about 6% for hourly jobs and 17% for salaried. Hourly turnover here is driven more by seasons, shifts and commuting than by a few points of pay.

2. Look at past raises the strongest evidence a company has

Find sites or jobs that got a market adjustment and compare their turnover for the 12 months before and after with similar sites that didn’t, over the same months. The difference between the two changes is the effect of the raise, with the economy, the season and the labor market taken out.

3. Ask the people who leave checks the direction

Code every exit interview and stay interview: pay, manager, schedule, commute, growth. If pay is the reason for one leaver in ten, a raise can only touch that tenth. It also shows where the money matters most.

4. Check it against research keeps the number honest

Studies of real pay changes find workers are quite sensitive to pay: Dube, Giuliano and Leonard (American Economic Review, 2019) found large quit responses at a US retailer, and Bassier, Dube and Naidu put the typical effect near 4, meaning about 20% fewer quits for a 5% raise. 15% sits just under that, which is why it is a fair starting point.

Three cautions
  • It doesn’t stretch. The first 5% above a weak position buys the most. Going from 1.05 to 1.10 buys less, and nobody stays forever, so the tool caps the effect at 60%.
  • Pay isn’t the only cause. Jobs that pay more are often also better jobs. Comparing within a family and level, and using past raises, separates the two.
  • Measure, then update. Put the number from method 1 in the box above in month two, and the one from method 2 once a year of results is in.
